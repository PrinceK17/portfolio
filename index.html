Who is Prince Kelvin?

I'm a writer who believes words should do more than inform, they should move people to care. I write articles, blog posts, scripts, fiction, and ghostwritten content that connect with real people, in real ways, about real things.

On Substack, I write as Jedspeare, exploring love, identity, self-worth, and the quiet tensions of being human. That's where my voice lives most honestly. But this portfolio is where that voice meets craft and strategy.

I'm a software engineering undergraduate at Babcock University, currently interning at Mavenaide Agency as a social media intern where I build content strategy and calendars. I took on a 30-day writing challenge, publishing every single day on Substack while balancing school and work. I delivered. And I loved every demanding moment of it.

What I care about most is making the reader feel something. That's the job.

Why Men Are Silent… And What It Costs Us
Article
Client Brief
Client: Marcus Oyelaran, Founder, "Grounded" (men's mental health platform) 800–1000 word article for Medium and platform blog. Tone: conversational, not a lecture. Intelligent but not academic. Men should feel seen, not preached at. Include at least two real statistics. End with something that challenges the reader, no fluffy conclusions.
Delivered Piece
He said, “I’m fine.” And everyone believed him. The conversation moved on, nothing seemed out of place… except the fact that he wasn’t fine, not even close, and the scary thing about it is how normal that has become, how easy it is for something so untrue to pass through a room without being questioned, without anyone pausing long enough to notice that sometimes “I’m fine” is just a way to end a conversation before it gets too real. Society makes us feel that a man’s pain is something that should be kept under control, managed quietly, handled in a way that doesn’t spill into other people’s space, as though struggling is only acceptable if it remains invisible, as though there is something wrong with being seen in the middle of not having it all together. Because it’s not that men don’t feel, and that’s the part that gets overlooked too often. We feel, and we feel deeply, but depth without expression doesn’t turn into understanding, it turns into weight, and weight that has nowhere to go doesn’t just disappear, it stays, it settles, it builds in ways that are hard to explain but even harder to ignore. The problem is that we, men, were never taught how to express what we feel, not in a way that felt natural, not in a way that felt safe, so even when the emotions are there, the words are not, and when you don’t have the words, you start to rely on silence, not because silence is better, but because it is familiar. Before we’re even born, society has already set the standards a man should follow, be strong, don’t cry, handle it yourself, don’t be soft, and over time that stops sounding like advice and starts becoming something else entirely, something you measure yourself against without even realizing it, something that quietly shapes how you respond to your own emotions. Vulnerability starts to feel like failure, and emotions start to feel like something that needs to be controlled rather than understood, so even when there is something real to say, something honest, it gets filtered, reduced, or avoided altogether because saying it out loud feels like stepping outside of what you’re supposed to be. So instead of saying, “I’m overwhelmed,” it comes out in other ways, in irritation that doesn’t match the situation, in distance that wasn’t intentional but still happens, in silence during moments that actually needed honesty, because when something isn’t expressed directly, it doesn’t disappear, it just finds other ways to show up. Studies have shown that a significant number of men don’t have a single close friend they can confide in emotionally, not one, and that absence creates a kind of isolation that isn’t always visible from the outside, because you can still be around people, still laugh, still show up, and yet have no real outlet for the things that actually matter. We’d rather die than feel, not always in the literal sense, but in the choices we make every day, choosing distraction over reflection, avoidance over confrontation, silence over expression, and the more those choices repeat themselves, the more natural they begin to feel, until it no longer feels like something you’re doing, but just the way things are. And society reinforces it in subtle and obvious ways, men who open up are often dismissed, mocked, or misunderstood, and even when it isn’t said directly, it is felt, so silence starts to seem safer, not because it is harmless, but because it protects you from being seen in a way that might not be accepted. But silence isn’t free, and even when it feels like the easier option, it carries a cost that builds over time. It costs inner peace, because unspoken emotions don’t disappear, they accumulate, and what accumulates begins to affect how you think, how you respond, how you exist within yourself, making it harder to feel settled, harder to feel clear, even when you can’t immediately explain why. It costs relationships, because you can be present and still be distant, involved and still unavailable in the ways that matter, and that’s why so many friendships between men stay on the surface, not because there’s no desire for something deeper, but because there’s no real practice of going there. It costs identity, because when you can’t express what you feel, you slowly lose connection with it, and when you lose connection with it, you start to lose connection with yourself, becoming more of what is expected and less of what is actually true. And in the worst cases, it costs lives, because men account for about 75% or more of suicide deaths in many countries, despite being less likely to be diagnosed with mental health conditions, which says more about how things are hidden than how things are felt. This isn’t weakness or stubbornness or conditioning, it’s a lack of safe spaces, a lack of environments where honesty can exist without being turned into something to be judged or dismissed, and when those spaces don’t exist, silence becomes the default. And maybe the harder question isn’t why men are silent, maybe it’s what that silence has already taken from you, the conversations you avoided, the friendships that never went deeper, the moments you wanted to say something real and didn’t, because those moments don’t just disappear, they stay in ways that are easy to overlook but hard to undo. Men need to start creating safe spaces for themselves, not perfect ones, not easy ones, but real ones where something honest can be said without it being immediately shut down or turned into something else. That kind of space doesn’t appear overnight, it’s built over time, in conversations that might feel unfamiliar at first, in moments where you choose to say something real even when it doesn’t come out perfectly, even when it feels like you’re stepping outside of what you’ve always known. Because if nothing changes, then the next time someone says, “I’m fine,” there’s a chance they won’t be here long enough for anyone to question it. And that’s the part we don’t like to sit with.

There's a quiet kind of pressure that has crept into modern life, especially for millennials. Somewhere between hustle culture, self improvement podcasts and becoming "that person" routines, mornings stopped being a beginning and started feeling like a performance. You wake up and before your feet even touch the ground, something is already waiting for you. Not physically. But you feel it. Journal. Meditate. Cold shower. Workout. Green smoothie. No phone. Affirmations. It's not like anyone is standing in your room telling you to do these things, but it feels like if you don't… you've already started the day wrong. And that's the strange part. Like you've stepped back into your own life. You are not a system to be optimized. You're allowed to wake up slowly. You're allowed to exist in your morning without turning it into something productive. And strangely, that's what makes it sustainable. Not discipline. Not pressure. Just the quiet willingness to return to something that feels good. Maybe your morning doesn't need to prove anything. Maybe it just needs to be yours. And you get to arrive in it… as you are.
Spec Work · Tolu / Lagos Anthology
The Danfo Didn't Move for Twenty-Three Minutes
Fiction
Client Brief
Client: Tolu, Creative Director, Lagos Short Story Anthology 600–900 word fiction piece set in Lagos, the island, mainland, a go-slow, wherever feels true. Theme: a moment where a character realises something they've been avoiding. Lagos must feel real, not like a postcard. The emotional turn must land.
Delivered Piece
The danfo had not moved in twenty-three minutes. Not that anyone was counting, but everyone knew. The air inside had turned thick, a mixture of sweat, perfume that had long lost its fight, and the faint, stubborn smell of petrol that clung to Lagos like a second skin. Someone behind him was arguing on the phone in rapid Yoruba, another person was tapping their foot like it could somehow push the bus forward, and the conductor had gone quiet, an unusual surrender. Kelvin sat by the window, elbow pressed against the rusted frame, watching the same yellow bus ahead of them like it might suddenly grow wings. It didn't. A hawker appeared, as they always do when the city pauses. Gala. Pure water. Plantain chips. The rhythm of survival. She moved from window to window with a tired confidence, her voice softer than usual. "Fine boy, buy something na." He almost laughed. Fine boy. If only she knew. He shook his head and looked away, but not before their eyes met for a second too long. There was something in her gaze, not pity, not quite indifference either. Just recognition. The kind that says, we're all just managing here. He had told her he was on his way. That was the last message he sent. "I'm coming. Let's talk." It had sounded brave when he typed it. Decisive. Like someone who had finally chosen something. But now, stuck in this unmoving bus, the words felt heavy in his chest. He had been avoiding this moment for months. Not just the conversation. Not just her. Himself. Lagos made that easy. The city rewards distraction. There is always somewhere to go, something to chase, someone to become. But here, in this forced stillness, there was nowhere to hide. He thought about the last time they were together. How she had looked at him, not angry, not even disappointed. Just… tired. "Kelvin," she had said softly, "what do you want?" Such a simple question. He had laughed it off. He had even made a joke. She didn't laugh. Because the truth was, he knew. He had always known. He just never stayed long enough in the moment to admit it. He had mastered the art of almost, almost saying it, almost showing up, almost choosing. And somehow, almost had started to feel like enough. What was he really afraid of? Not her reaction. Not the outcome. It was himself… after. The version of him that wouldn't have anywhere to hide. No more "I'm still figuring things out." No more stretching things into something undefined. Just him. Clear. Seen. Expected to show up. He didn't want to lose her. Not in the casual way. But in the quiet, unsettling way that made his chest tighten whenever he imagined her moving on, building something real with someone else. Because choosing her meant choosing a version of himself he wasn't sure he could live up to. It meant growing up. The bus picked up speed, finally breaking free from the go-slow. The wind rushed in through the open window, carrying with it the smell of rain-soaked asphalt, damp metal, and roasted corn from somewhere down the road. He wasn't ready. But he was done pretending he didn't know. And maybe, for the first time in a long time, that was enough. "Oshodi! Who dey drop?" Kelvin opened his eyes. He wasn't there yet. But he was closer than he had been before.

Why Indy’s Art Scene is Quietly Thriving.

When people think of major art cities in the United States, places like New York or Los Angeles usually come to mind. But in the heart of the Midwest, Indianapolis is building something quieter and surprisingly vibrant. In recent years, Indianapolis’ art scene has begun to experience a noticeable shift. What was once a quieter, more localized creative community is now gaining momentum, driven by a mix of emerging artists, accessible spaces, and a growing appetite for cultural expression. Part of this growth can be traced to a wider post-pandemic creative resurgence, where more individuals have turned to art as both expression and opportunity. Unlike larger cities such as New York City or Los Angeles, Indianapolis offers a more affordable and less saturated environment, giving artists room to experiment without the pressure of high costs or intense competition. This has allowed a more open and exploratory creative culture to take shape. At the same time, a wave of independent galleries, pop-up exhibitions, and community-led initiatives has made the scene more accessible. Spaces like Gallery 924 and the Harrison Center reflect this shift, acting as platforms where emerging and established artists can share work without needing major institutional backing. These spaces are not just showcasing art, they are building connections, encouraging collaboration, and slowly redefining what a local art ecosystem can look like. Events like the BUTTER Fine Art Fair also highlight how the city is evolving. With a stronger focus on artist ownership and accessibility, it represents a growing effort to make art more sustainable for the people creating it, not just the people viewing it.

The Artists, the Spaces, and the Community.

A big part of what is driving this quiet rise is the artists themselves. There is a growing number of local creatives who are choosing to stay in Indianapolis instead of relocating to bigger cities. Many of them are experimenting with different styles, mediums, and storytelling approaches, creating work that feels personal but still connected to wider cultural conversations. Alongside them, creative spaces are also expanding in small but meaningful ways. Independent galleries, studio collectives, and pop-up exhibitions are giving artists more places to show their work without needing major institutional backing. It is less about prestige and more about access, which is changing who gets to be seen. Then there is the community side of it. Art in Indianapolis is not just happening behind gallery walls. It is showing up in public murals, local events, and long-running cultural initiatives like Art & Soul, which has helped shape the city’s creative identity for decades. There is a sense that art is becoming part of everyday life in the city rather than something separate from it. Together, these elements are slowly shaping a more connected and active creative culture. In many ways, Indianapolis is not trying to compete with larger art cities. Instead, it is building something more grounded and quietly self-sustaining, that is exactly why it is starting to stand out.

What Makes Indy Different.

What stands out about Indy is not just that the art scene is growing, but how it is growing. Unlike bigger cities where creativity often feels tied to visibility, branding, or commercial success, Indy’s scene feels more personal and grounded. There is less pressure to “make it” in a traditional sense, and more room to simply create. This slower, more intentional pace has shaped the kind of art coming out of the city. A lot of it feels raw in a good way, less filtered, more honest, and often closely tied to lived experience. Instead of chasing global recognition, many artists here are focused on building something within their own community first. There is also a kind of intimacy to the scene that is hard to replicate in larger cities. People are not just attending exhibitions or passing through events, they are often directly connected to the artists, the spaces, and the stories behind the work. That creates a sense of familiarity that makes the art feel closer to everyday life rather than something distant or exclusive. In that sense, Indianapolis is not trying to compete with major art capitals. It is carving out its own identity quietly, steadily, and in a way that feels sustainable. And maybe that is what makes it stand out the most.

Looking Ahead.

The city’s art scene does not feel like something that has reached its peak. If anything, it still feels like it is in the early stages of defining itself. There is a steady sense of momentum building beneath the surface, carried by artists who are choosing to stay, spaces that are continuing to open, and a community that is slowly learning to pay more attention to its own creative voice. As more independent initiatives take shape and more people begin to engage with local art in everyday ways, the city’s creative identity is likely to become even more visible. Not in a loud or forced way, but in a gradual unfolding that feels natural to the city itself. What is happening is not a sudden rise, but a quiet expansion, and that might be its strongest advantage. It has time to grow without losing its character. In a landscape where many art scenes are competing for attention, Indianapolis is doing something different. It is simply building, and that is what matters. That is what gives the city its quiet sense of life.
